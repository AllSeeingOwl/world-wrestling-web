On 3 June 1994, at the Nippon Budokan in Tokyo, Mitsuharu Misawa defended the All Japan Pro Wrestling (AJPW) Triple Crown Heavyweight Championship against Toshiaki Kawada. Misawa retained after 35 minutes and 50 seconds with a Tiger Driver '91. The match is widely regarded as the high point of All Japan's 1990s style and one of the finest matches in the history of the craft.

## The Context

In the early 1990s, AJPW built its main events around four wrestlers later known as the "Four Pillars": Misawa, Kawada, Kenta Kobashi and Akira Taue. Kawada had been one of Misawa's closest allies in the Super Generation Army before breaking away to form the Holy Demon Army with Taue. The split gave their singles matches a personal dimension that the audience understood without needing it spelled out.

This was the third time Kawada had challenged Misawa for the Triple Crown during Misawa's reign. Each previous match ended in defeat for Kawada, so the crowd arrived knowing both the history and the stakes.

## The Match

The match was built on escalation. Holds and strikes from earlier encounters returned with greater force, and each wrestler survived moves that would have ended a lesser match. Kawada's stiff kicks and Misawa's elbow strikes became the language of the bout, and the near-falls grew more desperate as the match went on. Misawa finally put Kawada away with a running elbow and the Tiger Driver '91.

## Why It Matters

The match is a clear example of the "Universal Language" test in the registry's charter: its story of an underdog challenger who cannot quite overcome the champion is fully understandable without translated commentary. It demonstrated how long-term storytelling across several matches could pay off in a single night.

Its influence is still visible. The "King's Road" approach it exemplified, with escalating near-falls and a clear sense of hierarchy, shaped later generations of wrestlers in Japan and abroad. Its legacy also includes a cautionary side: the heavy head-drops and strikes common in this era are now part of wider discussions about performer safety.
